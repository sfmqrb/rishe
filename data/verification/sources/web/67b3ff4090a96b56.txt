 English

 Etymology
 [ edit ] 

 From Latin vōx ( “ voice ” ) . Doublet of voice .

 vox ( plural voxes or voces ) 

 ( music ) The voice , especially one's singing voice; vocals .

 Latin
 [ edit ] 

 Etymology
 [ edit ] 

 From Proto-Italic *wōks , from Proto-Indo-European *wṓkʷs ( “ speech, voice ” ) (with stem vōc- for voqu- from the nominative case), an o-grade root noun of *wekʷ- ( “ to speak ” ) . Cognates include Sanskrit वाच् ( vā́c ) and Ancient Greek ὄψ ( óps ) . [ 1 ] 

 Pronunciation
 [ edit ] 

 ( Classical Latin ) IPA ( key ) : [ˈwoːks] 

 ( modern Italianate Ecclesiastical ) IPA ( key ) : [ˈvɔks] 

 Noun
 [ edit ] 

 vōx   f ( genitive vōcis ) ; third declension 

 voice 
 c. 52 BCE , Julius Caesar , Commentarii de bello Gallico VII.17 : Nulla tamen vox est ab eis audita Although no voice was heard from them 

 accent 

 speech , remark , expression , (turn of) phrase 
 63 BCE , Cicero , In Catilinam orationes 1.29 : Hīs ego sānctissimīs reī pūblicae vōcibus et eōrum hominum, quī hoc idem sentiunt, mentibus paucā respondēbō. I will respond with a few thoughts to these most sacred remarks of the Republic, and to those men who feel the same way.
(Cicero has just concluded a section of the speech featuring the imaginary comments of a personified Roman Republic.) 

 word 

 ( grammar ) voice ; indicating the relation of the subject of the verb to the action which the verb expresses
