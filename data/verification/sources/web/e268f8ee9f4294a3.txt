 Ancient Greek
 [ edit ] 

 Etymology
 [ edit ] 

 From Proto-Indo-European *weḱ- ( “ to choose; to want ” ) . According to Beekes, the term derives from a lost present verb *ϝέκμῐ ( *wékmĭ ) , itself perhaps from a present verb equivalent to Sanskrit वष्टि॑ ( váṣṭi , “ to will ” ) and Hittite [Term?] ( /⁠wekkmi⁠/ , “ to want; to desire ” ) . [ 1 ] The LIV suggests that the Hittite and Greek forms derive from a single Narten-type present *wḗḱ-ti whereas the Sanskrit form supposedly—according to the LIV—derives from an athematic root present *wéḱti . [ 2 ] 

 Akin to Old Armenian վասն ( vasn , “ for the sake of ” ) and Old Persian 𐎺𐏁𐎴𐎠 ( vašnā , “ thanks to ” ) .

 Adjective
 [ edit ] 

 ἑκών • ( hekṓn )  m ( feminine ἑκοῦσα , neuter ἑκόν ) ; first / third declension 

 willing 
 Antonym: ᾱ̓́κων ( ā́kōn ) 
 460 BCE – 430 BCE , Herodotus , Histories 2.65.5 : [ … ] τὸ δ’ ἄν τις τῶν θηρίων τούτων ἀποκτείνῃ, ἢν μὲν ἑκών , θάνατος ἡ ζημίη, ἢν δὲ ἀέκων, ἀποτίνει ζημίην τὴν ἂν οἱ ἱρέες τάξωνται. [ … ] tò d’ án tis tôn thēríōn toútōn apokteínēi, ḕn mèn hekṓn , thánatos hē zēmíē, ḕn dè aékōn, apotínei zēmíēn tḕn àn hoi hirées táxōntai. Translation by George Rawlinson When a man has killed one of the sacred animals, if he did it with malice prepense , he is punished with death; if unwittingly, he has to pay such a fine as the priests choose to impose.
