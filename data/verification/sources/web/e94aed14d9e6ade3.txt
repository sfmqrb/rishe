 Origin and history of lark 

 lark (n.1) 

 small songbird of the Old World, known for its soaring and singing, early 14c., earlier lauerche (c. 1200), from Old English lawerce (late Old English laferce ), from Proto-Germanic *laiw(a)rikon (source also of Old Saxon lewerka , Frisian liurk , Old Norse lævirik , Dutch leeuwerik , German Lerche ), a word of unknown origin.

 The Old English and Old Norse forms suggest a contracted compound, perhaps meaning "treason-worker," but "nothing is known in folklore to account for such a designation" [OED, 1989]. Noted for its early song and high flying (in contrast to its ground nest). When the sky falls, we shall catch larks was an old proverb mocking foolish optimism.

 Latin alauda "the lark" (source of Italian aloda , Spanish alondra , Provençal alauza , Old French aloe ) is said to be from Gaulish (Celtic). True Latin names for the skylark were galerita , corydalus .

 SPLIT the Lark - and you’ll find the music -

 Bulb after Bulb, in Silver rolled

 [Emily Dickinson] 
 also from early 14c. 

 lark (n.2) 

 "spree, frolic, merry adventure," 1811, slang, of uncertain origin. Possibly a shortening of skylark (1809), sailors' slang for "play rough in the rigging of a ship" (larks were proverbial for high-flying). Or perhaps it is an alteration of English dialectal or colloquial lake / laik "to play, frolic, make sport" (c. 1300, from Old Norse leika "to play," reconstructed in Wakins to be ultimately from PIE *leig- (3) "to leap") with unetymological -r- common in southern British dialect.

 The verb lake , considered characteristic of Northern English vocabulary, is the opposite of work but lacks the other meanings of play . Related: Larky ; larkish (1821) . 
 also from 1811 

 lark (v.) 

 "to play tricks, frolic," 1813; see lark (n.2). Often with about . Related: Larked ; larking .
 also from 1813 

 Entries linking to lark 

 larking (n.) 

 "fun, frolicking," 1813, verbal noun from lark (v.); also see lark (n.2).

 larky (adj.) 

 "frolicsome," by 1841, from lark (n.2) + -y (2). Related: Larkiness . In U.S. 1830s my larky was a taunting phrase for a person one has had the better of.

 "Now, my larkies," said Jake. "You're fixed as slick as nothing. By Jupiter, but you're tough articles though. I won't want a fight for a week. You are now prisoners, and to morrow hey for the county jail!" ["An Interesting Story" in The Franklin Telegraph, Chambersburg, Pa., Nov. 6, 1832]
