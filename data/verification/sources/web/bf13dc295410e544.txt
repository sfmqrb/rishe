 From Wiktionary, the free dictionary

 This Proto-Indo-European entry contains reconstructed terms and roots . As such, the term(s) in this entry are not directly attested , but are hypothesized to have existed based on comparative evidence.

 Proto-Indo-European
 [ edit ] 

 Etymology
 [ edit ] 

 Borrowed from or into Proto-Semitic *ṯawr- ( “ bull, ox ” ) , or both originated from a common unknown source. [ 1 ] [ 2 ] (The unconditioned /a/ suggests a non-Indo-European etymon.) [ 3 ] 

 Noun
 [ edit ] 

 *táwros   m ( non-ablauting ) [ 2 ] [ 4 ] [ 5 ] [ 6 ] [ 7 ] [ 8 ] 

 wild bull , aurochs 

 Alternative reconstructions
 [ edit ] 

 *th₂ewro- [ 9 ] 

 *teh₂wro- [ 10 ] 

 Reconstruction notes
 [ edit ] 

 Beekes posits a reconstructed form *teh₂wro- , though Schrijver suggests that a such a pre-form is incompatible with the Balto-Slavic accent. Alternatively, Derksen posits a PIE form *th₂ewro- , which Schrijver considers to be formally possible albeit unusual for PIE. Though, he suggests that—should the word be a borrowing—it may have been adapted as *th₂ewro- to avoid the unusual phoneme *a . [ 11 ] However, the linguist Paulus van Sluis argues that the only laryngeal compatible with the Germanic and Albanian evidence is *h₁ , which he suggests is in conflict with the Greek evidence. [ 1 ] Similarly, the philologist Robert Woodhouse suggests that the correspondence between Lithuanian taũras , Serbo-Croatian ту̑р , and the barytone Greek word ταῦρος ( taûros ) contradicts any proposed medial laryngeal. [ 12 ] 

 The EIEC separates Proto-Germanic *steuraz and Avestan 𐬯𐬙𐬀𐬊𐬭𐬀 ( staora , “ large domestic animal ” ) , for which it prescribes a different shared pre-form of the shape *stewros ( “ large (domestic) animal ” ) . [ 13 ] Kroonen, however, considers the Germanic forms *steuraz and *þeuraz to have been borrowed from the same substrate source with "irregular sound substitution." [ 14 ] 

 Kroonen suggests that Proto-Celtic *tarwos , Proto-Germanic *steuraz , and Proto-Germanic *þeuraz display an irregular formal relationship and dialectal variation that "proves" that, rather than shared inheritance from a PIE word, the wanderwort spread following the break-up of the IE family. [ 15 ] In further support of this notion of a later borrowing within a dialect continuum, the linguist Tom Hercules Davies adduces Avestan 𐬯𐬙𐬀𐬊𐬭𐬀 ( staora ) , which he considers to reflect an unusual phonological correspondence. [ 16 ] However, van Sluis argues that only Germanic showcases internal variation and the—according to van Sluis—regular correspondences between Indo-European branches such as Proto-Celtic *tarwos and Proto-Italic *tauros indicates an early borrowing into the IE languages. [ 1 ] The EIEC likewise calls the term "widespread and old in IE." [ 2 ] Though, there is evidence that Proto-Italic *tauros is itself a loanword, as it does not showcase the same metathesis of *-aur- > *-aru- found in terms such as parvus . [ 17 ] Yet, there are also possible exceptions to this rule in Italic, such as Latin īnstaurō . [ 1 ]
