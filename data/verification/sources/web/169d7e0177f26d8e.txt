 Latin
 [ edit ] 

 Pronunciation
 [ edit ] 

 ( Classical Latin ) IPA ( key ) : [ˈwɛn.tʊs] 

 ( modern Italianate Ecclesiastical ) IPA ( key ) : [ˈvɛn.tus] 

 Etymology 1
 [ edit ] 

 Inherited from Proto-Italic *wē̆ntos , from Proto-Indo-European *h₂wéh₁n̥ts ( “ blowing ” ) , present participle of *h₂weh₁- ( “ to blow ” ) .

 Etymology issues

 The term is often cited as an example of Osthoff's law, which predicts shortening of long vowels when followed by a resonant and then a consonant. Thus, the thematicized pre-form *h₂weh₁n̥tos could have yielded *wēntos , [ 1 ] which would have eventually shortened into *wentos . [ 2 ] However, the existence of Osthoff's law in Latin is disputed, and one alternative proposal suggests that *h₂weh₁n̥tos would have instead directly yielded *wentos . According to this theory, the laryngeal would disappear without any accompanying developments as, [ 3 ] in Proto-Indo-European, laryngeals did not induce compensatory lengthening when in coda position. [ 4 ] 

 Cognate and synonymous with English wind , Sanskrit वात॑ ( vā́ta ) , Avestan 𐬬𐬁𐬙𐬀 ( vāta ) , and Ancient Greek ᾰ̓είς ( ăeís ) . See also Latin vannus , from the same PIE root.

 Noun
 [ edit ] 

 ventus   m ( genitive ventī ) ; second declension 

 wind 
 29 BCE – 19 BCE , Vergilius , Aeneis 4.704-705 : [...] Omnis et ūna / dīlāpsus calor, atque in ventōs vīta recessit. And all at once her warmth faded away, and her life receded into the winds . 
(The death of Dido; the conclusion of Book 4. See the notes by Page, T.E., [1967], pg. 394: “‘Life,’ ‘breath,’ ‘spirit,’ anima , ἄνεμος are so closely connected in human speech that poets naturally speak of life passing ‘into the winds.’”) 

 Inscription, The Crown 2016–2023: Homō sapiēns nōn ūrīnat in ventum A wise man pisseth not against the wind 

 8 CE , Ovidius , Fasti 4.729–730 : Mōta dea est operīque favet: nāvālibus exit
puppis, habent ventōs iam mea vēla suōs. The goddess is moved and she favors the work: my ship is leaving the docks, already my sails have their winds . 
(Inspiration returns as, metaphorically, the poet and his readers sail onward. Idiomatically, Ovid ‘‘has found his second wind’’ while writing the fourth book of the Fasti .)

 Etymology 2
 [ edit ] 

 Inherited from Proto-Italic *gʷemtus , from Proto-Indo-European *gʷém-tu-s , from *gʷem- ( “ to come ” ) . The -n- was, according to De Vaan, potentially adopted from the verb veniō ( “ to come ” ) . By surface analysis , veniō +‎ -tus ( forming action nouns ) .

 Noun
 [ edit ] 

 ventus   m ( genitive ventūs ) ; fourth declension ( Old Latin , dubious hapax legomenon ) 

 a coming ; an arrival 
 116 BCE – 27 BCE , Marcus Terentius Varro , De Lingua Latina 5.72 (quoting Plautus, Cistellaria 14-15): Venilia a veniendo, ac ventu illo, quem Plautus dicit: Quod ille dixit, qui secundo vento vectus est tranquillo mari, ventum gaudeo. [ The theonym ] Venilia [ comes ] from veniō [ “to come” ] and ventus , which Plautus [ thus ] refers to: “As quoth the man who was carried by a favorable wind over a calm sea, ‘I rejoice at the wind's arrival [ or “I am glad that it breezed in “; a pun on the double meaning of the word ventus ] ’ ”.
