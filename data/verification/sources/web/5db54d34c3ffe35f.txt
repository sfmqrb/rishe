 Origin and history of tough 

 tough (adj.) 

 Middle English tough , from Old English toh "strong and firm in texture, flexible without being brittle; tenacious, sticky, not easily separated;" from Proto-Germanic *tanhu- (source also of Middle Low German tege , Middle Dutch taey , Dutch taai , Old High German zach , German zäh ), which Watkins suggests is from PIE *denk- "to bite," on the notion of "holding fast." See rough for the spelling.

 It is attested from c. 1200 as "strong, powerful," also, in reference to battle or contests, "stiff, severe, violent." It is from c. 1300 as "not tender or fragile, capable of great physical endurance, not easily overcome," and early 14c. as "difficult to chew," also "hard to endure."

 The figurative sense of "steadfast, not easily influenced, unyielding" is from mid-14c.; that of "hard to do, requiring great or continued effort, laborious" is from 1610s. As "resolute in dealing with opposition," by 1906; to get tough is by 1938, and get-tough as an adjective (with policy , etc.) is by 1978. Tough guy is attested from 1901. Tough-minded is recorded by 1907 (in William James).

 Of circumstances, implying hardship, injustice, etc., American English colloquial, attested by 1890. Tough luck is attested by 1912; tough shit , dismissive retort to a complaint, is attested by 1946.

 tough (n.) 

 "street ruffian, bully, person given to violent behavior," 1866, American English, from tough (adj.). Compare rough (n.).
 also from 1866 

 tough (v.) 

 in tough it "endure the experience," by 1830, American English colloquial, from tough (adj.).  Rough it "put up with hardships" is from 18c. (see rough (v.)).
 also from 1830 

 Entries linking to tough 

 rough (n.) 

 c. 1200, "broken ground, a rough surface," from rough (adj.). From 1640s as "the disagreeable side of anything." The meaning "a rowdy" is attested by 1837, but Century Dictionary calls this perhaps rather an abbreviation of ruffian conformed in spelling to rough . The specific sense in golf, in reference to the ground at the edge of the greens, is by 1901.

 Phrase in the rough "in an unfinished or unprocessed condition" (of timber, etc.) is from 1620s, in rough diamond "diamond in its natural state," which was used figuratively, of persons, by 1700, hence diamond in the rough (by 1874 of persons, in the figurative sense "one whose good character is somewhat masked by rough manners and want of education or style").

 taut (adj.) 

 mid-13c., tohte, tought "stretched or pulled tight, strained, not slack," possibly from tog- , past participle stem of Old English teon "to pull, drag," from Proto-Germanic *theuhanan , from PIE root *deuk- "to lead," which would connect it to tow (v.) and tie . But OED (1989) writes that "the history of this word is in many points obscure." Middle English Compendium finds it probably a special development from variant forms of tough (adj.) with possible influence from teon . Commonly confused with taunt . Related: Tautly; tautness .
