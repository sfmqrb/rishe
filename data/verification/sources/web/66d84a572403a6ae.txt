 اللّٰه ( allāh ) 

 Root 

 ء ل ه ( ʔ l h ) 

 10 terms 

 Alternative forms

 اللّاه ( allāh ) — misspelling , pronunciation spelling 

 Etymology

 Widely theorized to be a contraction of الٱِلٰه ( al-ilāh , “ God ” ) , from الإلٰه ( al-ʔilāh , “ the deity ” ) with loss of initial hamza after the definite article ال ( al- ) . Others suggest it is a variant spelling of the more ancient أَلّٰه ( ʔallāh ) , the extra ل ( l ) serving as an emphatic marker to emphasize distinction, first attested in the Mecca and Taif region, and later in the Quran. Both proposed roots were current in pre-Islamic usage, particularly الإلٰه ( al-ʔilāh ) in Nabataean macaronic Arabic-Aramaic usage from which the singular use has presumably spread. Ultimately from Proto-Semitic *ʔil- . Cognate with Classical Syriac ܐܰܠܳܗܳܐ ( ʾalāhā ) , Assyrian Neo-Aramaic ܐܲܠܵܗܵܐ ( allāhā ) , Aramaic אֱלָהָא ( ʾĕlāhā ) , Hebrew אֱלוֹהַּ ( ʾelōah ) , and Old South Arabian 𐩱𐩡𐩠 ( ʾlh ) .

 Usage notes

 In Islamic contexts, this word may alternatively be translated into English as Allah . Note, however, that the Arabic word is just as neutral and general as English God , and does not express any particularly Islamic notion at all. الله ( allāh ) is the Arabic word used by Christians, Jews, and other monotheists to describe the God of their own religions, and is cognate to the words used in Hebrew and Syriac. The Jewish sage Saadia Gaon even used الله ( allāh ) to translate the Tetragrammaton in his Arabic translation of the Torah.

 ( monotheism ) God

 هو ملحد، ما بيآمن بشي إسمو الله huwwe mulḥid, ma biʔāmin bi šī ʔismo ʔaḷḷa . He’s an atheist, he doesn’t believe in such a thing as God .

 Usage notes

 Used for God by all or almost all religions of the Levant, including Islam , Christianity , Druzism , Alawism , and (inasmuch as Jews continue to speak Levantine Judeo-Arabic dialects) Judaism , as well as other minority religions. Some religions have additional terms for God specific to their traditions.
